• The light had been in Thomas Kell's family for sixty years, first his father, then him. The tower stood on a rock called Skerrinish, eight hundred
  meters end to end at low tide, and the sea owned everything else. Thomas kept the lamp lit from one hour before dusk to one hour after dawn, every
  night, in every kind of weather, for thirty-one years.

  His mornings began the same way. He climbed the ninety-three steps to the lantern room, wound the clockwork that turned the lens, wiped the glass
  with a rag dampened with vinegar, and checked the mercury bath that let the lens float and spin. The lamp itself had been converted to electric in
  1971, but the old habits stayed. His father had taught him to trim a wick by feel, and Thomas could still do it with his eyes closed, though there
  was no wick to trim. He did it anyway, some evenings, just to feel the shape of the work.

  The island was not really an island in good weather. A causeway of rock and shingle surfaced at half tide, and the supply boat came every second
  Tuesday with diesel, tinned food, paraffin for the stove, and whatever Thomas had written on the order form the week before. He wrote the same
  things most weeks: bread flour, tea, mackerel hooks, a newspaper he was usually two weeks behind in reading. The boat's skipper, a man named Iain,
  had been making the run since Thomas was a boy, and the two of them had worn through most topics of conversation years ago. What remained was a kind
  of companionable silence, the two of them sitting in the wheelhouse with tea, watching the water, saying nothing, agreeing with it.

  Thomas had married once, in his thirties, to a woman from the mainland named Bridget. She lasted four winters. It was not the loneliness, she said
  afterward on the pier at Mallaig. It was the wind. It got into the house the way water gets into stone, and it never left. Thomas understood. He had
  stopped hearing it years before she arrived. She took the ferry and did not come back, and he sent her a card every Christmas, and she sent one
  back, and that was the whole of the marriage as it continued.

  In February of his thirty-first year, the brig Hjördis came out of the dark. She was Danish, old, carrying timber, and the weather that night was
  the kind Thomas called honest — a full gale from the southwest, steady, no tricks in it, the kind you could set a watch by. The light was working.
  It had never once failed on his watch. But the Hjördis had lost her steering a mile out, and the sea was doing what it wanted with her.
