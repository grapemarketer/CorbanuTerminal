  stopped hearing it years before she arrived. She took the ferry and did not come back, and he sent her a card every Christmas, and she sent one
  back, and that was the whole of the marriage as it continued.

  In February of his thirty-first year, the brig Hjördis came out of the dark. She was Danish, old, carrying timber, and the weather that night was
  the kind Thomas called honest — a full gale from the southwest, steady, no tricks in it, the kind you could set a watch by. The light was working.
  It had never once failed on his watch. But the Hjördis had lost her steering a mile out, and the sea was doing what it wanted with her.

  Thomas saw her masthead light wander across the line of the beam and then turn, slowly, toward the shore north of the tower. He was out the door in
  oilskins and boots before he had finished thinking about it. The causeway was under water. He went along the cliff path instead, the lamp above him
  swinging its white sector over his head every four seconds, and he watched her strike. It was not a great crash. It was a long grinding sound,
  timber on rock, going on much longer than seemed possible.

  He had a rope coiled at the top of the path, part of the station's old line-throwing gear, and he waited for a shape in the water and threw. He was
  forty-two then and still strong. He pulled four men out of the surf that night, one after another, hauling them up the cliff face with his arms and
  the rope and nothing else. A fifth went under and did not come up. Thomas searched the rocks below the tower for three days afterward, and Iain came
  out on the boat and helped, and they never found him. The other four sat in Thomas's kitchen in blankets, drinking tea, not speaking, until the
  ferry came.

  The Danish consulate sent a letter. The widow of the man who was lost sent one too. Thomas kept both in the drawer under the tide tables, where he
  kept the things he did not want to look at and could not throw away.

  He retired at sixty-five, when the Northern Lighthouse Board automated the station. The last night, he climbed the steps, wound a mechanism that no
  longer needed winding, and sat in the lantern room while the lens turned above him. Then he went down the ninety-three steps and did not look back.
